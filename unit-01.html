Unit 1

Number systems and operations

Sessions 1–3  |  31 August – 11 September  |  Worksheet 3

Everything later in this course sits on top of this unit. Nothing here is new to you — you have counted, measured, split a bag and worked out a percentage for years. What is new is the names, and knowing exactly when each kind of number is needed.

1.1 The kinds of number

Four questions from one day on a farm.

How many goats are in the pen? The thermometer reads three below the mark — what temperature is that? Three-quarters of a bag went on the first field — how much is left? A square plot covers exactly one hectare — how long is one side?

Each answer is a number, but they are not all the same kind of number. That is what this section is about.

The sets fit inside one another, each one bigger than the last:

The sets, from smallest outwards

Natural numbers — 1, 2, 3, …   Counting. Goats, bags, rows.

Whole numbers — the naturals plus 0. An empty store is zero, not nothing.

Integers — the wholes plus the negatives. A temperature below the mark, a loss on a sale.

Rational numbers — anything you can write as one integer over another. Three-quarters of a bag, 2.5 kg per plot, 0.75 of a hectare.

Irrational numbers — decimals that never end and never repeat. The side of a one-hectare square is 100 m, but the diagonal across it is 141.42135… m, going on forever.

Real numbers — all of the above together.

Two things worth fixing in your mind now: a decimal that recurs, like 0.333…, is rational — it is 1/3. And not every square root is irrational: √49 is just 7.

1.2 How the operations behave

Three fields, each needing $40 of fertiliser and $25 of seed.

You can cost it as three lots of $65, or as three lots of $40 plus three lots of $25. Both give $195. You have been relying on that for years; it has a name.

The five properties

Commutative — order does not matter. 7 × $12 is the same as 12 × $7. True for adding and multiplying. Not for subtracting or dividing: $20 − $8 is not $8 − $20.

Associative — grouping does not matter. Add a column of prices in any grouping and the total is the same.

Distributive — 3($40 + $25) = 3 × $40 + 3 × $25. This is the one you will use most in this course. It is also how brackets get multiplied out.

Identity — adding 0 or multiplying by 1 changes nothing.

Inverse — every operation has something that undoes it. Owing $50 and paying $50. Doubling, then halving. This is exactly what you do when you solve an equation.

Closure is a different idea. A set is closed under an operation if the answer always stays inside that set.

Worked example

Are the natural numbers closed under subtraction?

4 and 9 are both natural numbers.

But 4 − 9 = −5, which is not a natural number.

So no. One counter-example settles it. This is precisely why negative numbers had to be invented — to record a loss.

1.3 Signed numbers and the order of operations

You have $40 in the account and spend $65.

Nobody needs a rule to know where that leaves you. The rules below are only a way of writing down what you already do.

The signs, built from a pattern

3 × (−5) = −15

2 × (−5) = −10

1 × (−5) = −5

0 × (−5) = 0

−1 × (−5) = +5

Each line goes up by 5. The pattern cannot do anything else, so two negatives must give a positive. You never have to remember the rule — you can rebuild it in ten seconds.

Order of operations

What is 6 + 2 × 5?

Two answers are possible — 40 if you work left to right, 16 if you multiply first. A bill, a contract and a calculator all need one agreed answer, so there is a convention:

Brackets, then powers, then multiply and divide (left to right), then add and subtract (left to right).

6 + 2 × 5 = 6 + 10 = 16

1.4 Fractions, decimals and percentages

How many half-bags fit into three-quarters of a bag?

You can answer that without any method at all. Say the answer out loud, then look at how it is written down — the writing will make far more sense in that order.

Adding: you cannot add quarters to sixths

3/4 + 1/6

Change both to twelfths:   9/12 + 2/12

= 11/12

A common denominator is just a change of unit, the same as converting kilograms to grams before adding.

Dividing: “how many of these fit into that”

3/4 ÷ 1/2   — how many half-bags fit into three-quarters of a bag?

One and a half. You knew that before any method.

The method: invert the second fraction and multiply.   3/4 × 2/1 = 6/4 = 3/2, which is 1½.

Percentages: estimate first

Find 15% of $340.

10% is $34, so the answer is a bit over $50. Estimating first catches every decimal-point error.

0.15 × 340 = $51

Watch out for these

Adding fractions by adding the tops and the bottoms
1/2 + 1/2 would come out as 2/4, which is a half. Test any method on something you already know the answer to.
Losing the sign when a negative is in front of a bracket
This is the most expensive single error in the whole course. Write the multiplication out term by term rather than doing it in your head.
Working an expression strictly left to right
6 + 2 × 5 is 16, not 40. Multiplication and division come before addition and subtraction.
Reading 0.5% as 0.5
Per cent means per hundred, so 0.5% is 0.005. Half a percent of $400 cannot possibly be $200.
Assuming a 20% rise then a 20% fall gets you back
$500 up 20% is $600; $600 down 20% is $480. The fall is taken on the larger amount, so it removes more than the rise added.
Thinking every square root is irrational
√49 is 7 and √0.25 is 0.5. Work out the value before you classify it.

Before you move on

You should be able to say which kind of number you are dealing with, handle signed numbers and brackets without hesitating, and move between a fraction, a decimal and a percentage in either direction.
